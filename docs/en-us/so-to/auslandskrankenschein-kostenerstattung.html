Getting Reimbursed for Foreign Medical Treatment: How It Works

When you fall ill or get injured while traveling, you are often asked to pay out-of-pocket, even though you have health insurance in the US. After returning home, you can often recover some or all of the costs. Three questions are decisive: Where were you treated, how are you insured, and what documentation do you have? The generator above walks you through exactly these points and creates a factual claim letter from them.

Domestic Health Insurance: Coverage Abroad

Most US employer-sponsored plans and Affordable Care Act (ACA) marketplace plans offer very limited coverage outside the United States. Some plans provide emergency care coverage abroad, but routine care is typically excluded. If a doctor or hospital requires upfront payment or is out-of-network, you can submit the costs to your insurer for reimbursement after returning home. The legal framework is governed by your specific plan documents, ERISA (for employer plans), and state insurance regulations.

Countries with Reciprocal Agreements

The United States has limited reciprocal healthcare agreements with certain countries (e.g., under specific circumstances in the UK). Medicare beneficiaries may receive limited coverage in very specific situations, such as when traveling through Canada between Alaska and another state, or if a foreign hospital is closer than the nearest US hospital for an emergency. Always verify with your plan administrator before traveling.

Why You Often Don't Get the Full Amount Back

Domestic health insurers typically reimburse only up to the "usual and customary" rate or a percentage of out-of-network charges for the region. Foreign private hospitals or specialists often bill significantly higher amounts. Deductibles, copays, and coinsurance still apply. You may be able to claim the difference through a travel medical insurance policy if its terms allow.

Travel Medical Insurance or Medicare/Medicaid

Travel medical insurance and government programs perform according to their policy terms. Key factors are the coverage area, possible deductibles, and notification requirements. Many travel insurance plans require that a claim be reported immediately, and for inpatient stays, sometimes while you are still abroad. The original itemized bill with proof of payment is almost always required. Some travel policies only pay the portion that your domestic health insurance does not cover.

Documents You Should Have Ready

Original itemized bill with treatment date and service description, proof of payment, medical records or physician report, prescription receipts, and if applicable, a copy of your insurance card. Foreign language documents should be translated if possible, or at least have the essential information summarized. The insurer may request additional documentation.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) About Foreign Medical Reimbursement

Does my US health insurance cover medical treatment abroad?

Most domestic US health insurance plans (employer-sponsored, ACA marketplace plans) provide very limited coverage outside the country. Some plans cover emergency care abroad, but routine care is typically excluded. If you paid out-of-pocket, you can submit a claim to your insurer for potential reimbursement based on your plan's out-of-network or foreign emergency provisions.

What should I do if I had to pay out-of-pocket abroad without using my insurance card?

Keep the original itemized bill, proof of payment, and medical records. Submit these to your insurance company after returning home. Explain in your claim why the card could not be used. Whether and how much is reimbursed depends entirely on your specific plan's terms.

Why might I not receive the full amount I paid?

Health insurers typically reimburse based on 'usual and customary' rates or a percentage of out-of-network charges. Foreign private hospitals often bill significantly higher amounts. Deductibles, copays, and coinsurance still apply. A separate travel medical insurance policy may cover the difference if its terms allow.

When does travel medical insurance pay instead of my regular health insurance?

Travel medical insurance is designed specifically for international trips and often covers emergency medical care, medical evacuation, and repatriation. It typically acts as secondary payer if you have domestic health insurance. Check your policy for coverage limits, pre-authorization requirements, and claim filing deadlines.

How long do I have to file a reimbursement claim?

Filing deadlines vary by insurance company and state regulations. Most US health plans require claims to be submitted within 90 days to 12 months of the service date. Travel insurance policies often have strict deadlines (e.g., 30-90 days after returning). File your claim as soon as possible after returning home.

Important Notes Before Submitting

Only submit copies unless the insurer explicitly requires originals, and keep a complete copy of your claim for your records. For larger amounts, call your insurer in advance to confirm exactly which documents they need. If denied, you typically have the right to file an internal appeal; the Explanation of Benefits (EOB) or denial letter will state the deadline and process.
