Getting Reimbursed for Foreign Medical Treatment: How It Works

When you fall ill or get injured whilst travelling, you are often asked to pay out-of-pocket, even though you are entitled to NHS care at home. After returning to the UK, you can often recover some or all of the costs. Three questions are decisive: where were you treated, what cover do you have, and what documentation do you have? The generator above walks you through exactly these points and creates a factual claim letter.

NHS Cover Abroad: GHIC, EHIC and Reciprocal Agreements

UK residents are entitled to state-provided medically necessary healthcare in the EU, Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein and Switzerland when using a valid GHIC (UK Global Health Insurance Card) or an existing EHIC (until its expiry date). You receive treatment on the same terms as a resident of that country, including any co-payments they would have to pay. If a doctor or hospital was private or your card could not be used, you can submit the costs to the NHS Overseas Healthcare Team for reimbursement after returning home.

Countries with Reciprocal Healthcare Agreements

The UK has reciprocal healthcare agreements with a small number of countries outside Europe (e.g. Australia, New Zealand and some British Overseas Territories). These agreements generally cover necessary healthcare during a temporary stay. Always check the latest guidance on NHS.uk before you travel, as entitlements vary by country.

Why You Often Do Not Get the Full Amount Back

The NHS only reimburses up to the cost that would have been covered by the state system of the country you were visiting. Private hospitals or doctors often charge significantly more than state rates. Co-payments that local residents would also have to pay are not reimbursed. You may be able to claim the difference through travel insurance or a Private Medical Insurance policy, if the terms allow.

Private Medical Insurance (PMI) or Travel Insurance

Private Medical Insurance and Travel Insurance pay according to their policy terms. Key factors are the geographical cover, any excess, and notification requirements. Travel insurance policies typically require that a claim be reported immediately, and for hospital admissions often whilst you are still abroad. The original itemised bill with proof of payment is almost always required. Many travel policies only pay what the NHS does not cover.

Documents You Should Have Ready

Original itemised bill with treatment date and description of services, proof of payment, medical report or hospital discharge summary, prescription receipts, and if applicable, a copy of your GHIC or EHIC. Foreign-language documents should be translated where possible, or at least have the essential information summarised. The NHS or insurer may request additional documentation.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) on Foreign Medical Reimbursement

Does the NHS cover medical treatment abroad?

The NHS does not normally pay for healthcare abroad, but UK residents can receive state-provided medically necessary treatment in EU countries, Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein and Switzerland using a valid GHIC or existing EHIC. If you paid out-of-pocket, you can apply for reimbursement through the NHS Overseas Healthcare Team.

What should I do if I had to pay out-of-pocket without using my GHIC/EHIC?

Keep the original itemised bill, proof of payment and any medical reports. Submit these to the NHS Overseas Healthcare Team after returning home. Explain in your claim why the card could not be used. Whether and how much is reimbursed is decided by the NHS team based on the state healthcare rates of the country you visited.

Why might I not receive the full amount I paid?

The NHS only reimburses up to the cost that would have been covered by the state system of the country you were in. Private hospitals or doctors often charge more than state rates. Any co-payments that local residents would also have to pay are not reimbursed. Travel insurance may cover the difference.

When does travel insurance pay instead of the NHS?

Travel insurance is designed for emergencies abroad and typically covers medical costs, repatriation, and cancellation. It often acts alongside the NHS – the NHS covers what it is obliged to, and travel insurance covers the rest (e.g. private treatment, air ambulance, repatriation). Always check your policy terms and notify your insurer promptly.

How long do I have to submit a reimbursement claim?

For NHS overseas reimbursement, you should submit your claim within 12 months of the treatment date, although later claims may still be considered in exceptional circumstances. Travel insurance policies usually have strict deadlines, often 30-90 days after returning home. Submit your claim as soon as possible.

Important Notes Before Submitting

Only submit copies unless the NHS or insurer explicitly requires originals, and keep a complete copy of your claim for your records. For larger amounts, contact the NHS Overseas Healthcare Team or your insurer in advance to confirm exactly which documents they need. If a claim is declined, you have the right to request a review or appeal; the decision letter will explain the process and any deadlines.
