Cancel a holiday: How to challenge excessive cancellation charges

A sudden illness, changed holiday plans or a family emergency – there are many reasons why you may need to cancel a booked package holiday. Alongside the disappointment of missing the trip, the next shock often follows quickly: the tour operator issues substantial cancellation charges. Providers often demand a flat 40%, 60% or even 80% of the holiday price, even where the cancellation is made weeks or months in advance.

What many travellers do not realise is this: standard cancellation charges cannot be set at any level the organiser likes. The law requires organisers to deduct the costs they save as a result of the cancellation, such as unused hotel nights or taxes. If your travel provider is using cancellations as a lucrative source of income, you may be able to challenge the demand and recover a significant part of your money.

Checklist: Cancel properly and reduce your costs

If cancelling your holiday is unavoidable, take a strategic approach to keep the financial loss as low as possible:

Checklist

  • Check the terms and conditions: Look in your booking documents for the cancellation scale (what percentage becomes payable at what stage?).
  • Cancel early: Do not wait. Every day counts. The earlier you cancel, the lower the contractual cancellation band will usually be.
  • Find a replacement traveller: Under the Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements Regulations 2018, you can generally transfer a package holiday booking to another person. Ask around to see whether someone can take over the trip.
  • Ask for evidence: Require the organiser in writing to set out its actual loss instead of simply accepting an excessive flat-rate charge. Places can often be resold.
  • Check your travel insurance: Review whether you took out cancellation cover when booking (or through your credit card) and whether an insured event applies.

Case studies: When the organiser demands too much

Example 1: Cancellation 5 months before departure

The Smith family books an expensive cruise but has to cancel it 5 months before sailing. Under its terms and conditions, the organiser demands a flat 40% of the holiday price as compensation.
Outcome: A charge that high so far in advance is often difficult to justify legally, because the cabin can usually be resold without difficulty. The family objects and asks for a calculation of the actual loss. The organiser backs down and reduces the charge to a 10% administration fee.

Example 2: The replacement traveller solution

Mr Brown breaks his leg shortly before his holiday to Majorca. His brother offers to go instead. The organiser says this counts as a full cancellation (80% charge) plus an expensive new booking at the current, higher daily price.
Outcome: That is unlawful. For a package holiday, the brother can generally take over the contract. The organiser may only charge the actual additional costs incurred by the transfer, for example the cost of changing the flight ticket. FAQ: Your rights when cancelling a holiday

What is the maximum amount a cancellation charge can be?
The level of cancellation charges is usually set out in the organiser’s terms and conditions and increases the closer you get to departure. However, the legal position is that the charges must be reasonable. The organiser may only claim the price less what it saves as a result of the cancellation or earns by reselling the holiday to someone else. Blanket demands for 80% or 90% well before departure are often unenforceable.
What happens if I provide a replacement traveller?
For a package holiday, under the Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements Regulations 2018, you generally have the right to transfer the booking to another person on reasonable notice before the holiday starts. In that case, the organiser cannot impose excessive cancellation charges, but may only charge the actual costs of making the change (often a modest administration fee of around £25 to £45).
When can I cancel a holiday completely free of charge?
You may be entitled to cancel without paying a termination fee if unavoidable and extraordinary circumstances occur at the destination or in its immediate vicinity and significantly affect the performance of the package or the carriage of passengers to the destination. Typical examples include war, serious natural disasters or dangerous pandemics, particularly where the Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO) has issued official travel advice against travel.
Do I have to pay cancellation charges if I have travel insurance?
If you cancel for an insured reason, such as an unexpected serious illness or redundancy, your travel insurance will usually cover the cancellation costs, less any applicable excess. However, many insurers will also check whether the charge demanded by the organiser is lawful and may refuse to pay excessive flat-rate charges. It is often worth challenging the organiser in those circumstances too, and our letters can help you do that.
