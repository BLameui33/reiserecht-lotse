Refused Refund? Initiate a Credit Card Chargeback

TL;DR (Quick Answer): If a travel provider refuses a refund for a canceled service, the Fair Credit Billing Act allows you to dispute the transaction with your credit card company. You generally have 60 days to act. Select your merchant below to generate a formal dispute letter for your bank.

If an airline cancels your flight or a tour operator goes bankrupt and refuses to issue a refund, your credit card is your best safety net. Under the Fair Credit Billing Act, US consumers have the right to dispute charges for services that were paid for but not delivered.

A "chargeback" allows your bank to forcibly reverse the transaction and return the money to your account. Our tool helps you generate a clear, structured document to submit to your credit card issuer (like Chase, Amex, or Citi) to prove your case.

Checklist: Filing a Successful Chargeback

Banks require proof before they take money back from a merchant. Follow these steps:

Chargeback Preparation List

  • Attempt resolution first: You must try to get a refund directly from the merchant before asking your bank for a chargeback.
  • Document the refusal: Save the email or chat transcript showing the merchant denying your refund or offering only a voucher you didn't want.
  • Identify the reason code: In travel scenarios, the dispute is almost always "Services Not Provided" or "Canceled Service".
  • Act quickly: Do not wait. You generally only have 60 days from your billing statement to file the dispute.

Case Studies: When to Dispute

Example: The Forced Travel Voucher

An airline cancels a flight to Europe and only offers the passenger a travel credit valid for one year. The passenger wants their money back, but the airline support hotline hangs up.
Solution: The passenger initiates a chargeback with their credit card company. Since DOT rules mandate cash refunds for canceled flights, the bank reverses the charge and the passenger gets their money back.

Example: The Bankrupt Tour Operator

A family books a $4,000 safari package. Two weeks before departure, the tour operator files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy and stops communicating. The money seems lost.
Solution: The family files a dispute under the reason code "Services Not Provided". Because the purchase was made on a credit card, the issuing bank steps in, recovers the funds, and protects the consumer from the bankruptcy loss.

Example: The Misrepresented Hotel (Not as Described)

A traveler books a luxury resort online. Upon arrival, the pool is under heavy construction (no prior warning), the room lacks AC, and conditions are unlivable. The hotel manager refuses to cancel the non-refundable booking.
Solution: The traveler documents the construction with photos, leaves the hotel, and books elsewhere. They file a chargeback for "Service Not as Described". With photographic proof, the bank rules in favor of the traveler.

FAQ: Chargeback Process

Will the merchant ban me for doing a chargeback?
It is possible. Some merchants (like certain ride-share apps or airlines) may suspend your account if you initiate a chargeback. You should only use this method if the merchant is unfairly withholding a refund you are entitled to, and you do not mind potentially losing access to that specific account.
What if I paid with a debit card?
Debit cards are governed by the Electronic Fund Transfer Act, which has different rules and tighter deadlines than credit cards. While you can still dispute debit card charges, the protection is often weaker and your actual bank account funds remain tied up during the investigation.

Chargeback Glossary (Key Terms)

  • Reason Code: A standardized alphanumeric code your bank uses to categorize why a charge is being disputed (e.g., Visa Reason Code 13.1 for Services Not Provided).
  • Acquiring Bank: The bank that processes credit card payments on behalf of the merchant.
  • FCBA: Fair Credit Billing Act – the 1974 US federal law that establishes your right to dispute billing errors and unfulfilled services.
